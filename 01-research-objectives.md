# 1. Research objectives and problem scope

Candidates stop asking for referrals before the process has had time to pay out, and the ones who do not stop lose the context that made each ask worth answering. Four research questions test that claim. Each carries a condition under which we abandon the problem statement it serves.

## The journey under study

A referral ask is not a single event. It is a thread that stays open across days or weeks and across two people who are never acting at the same time. Seven stages:

1. Deciding which roles to target
2. Finding an opening worth pursuing
3. Identifying who at that company could refer
4. Sending the request or the ask
5. Waiting, with no signal of any kind
6. Re-engaging when someone accepts or replies
7. Deciding whether to continue or to stop

Stages 5 to 7 are where this study expects to find the problem, and they are the stages a feature-led analysis skips. Most people studying this journey stop the map at stage 4, because sending is the last thing the candidate actively does.

## The two problem statements

### Idea A: the thread outlives the candidate's memory of it

The candidate sends a connection request today in order to ask about one specific opening. The counterpart accepts five days later. In that interval the candidate has sent twenty other requests, attended classes, submitted assignments and applied elsewhere. At the moment of acceptance, which is the moment the ask becomes possible, the candidate may no longer recall who the person is, which company they sit in, which role prompted the request, where the posting now is, or what was already said.

The cost is paid at the point of success rather than the point of failure. The invitation was accepted. That part worked. What fails is everything after it.

### Idea B: effort is front-loaded, feedback is delayed, so candidates quit on silence

Work per company arrives in one block and in sequence: locate the opening, identify the company, find people inside it who could refer, reach each of them, write to each of them. Feedback arrives later, if at all, and probabilistically. The candidate spends their available effort in two or three days, receives nothing in that window, reads the silence as rejection, and stops.

They stop before the process could have produced a result, so they never find out whether it works. The window in which they are deciding is also the window in which academic deadlines, placement preparation and everything else outside college are competing for the same attention.

### Why manual labour is not a third problem statement

The repetition in stages 2 to 4 is real, and it is the mechanism behind Idea B rather than a problem in its own right. Stated on its own, tedium names its own answer, which is automation, and the exercise then tests a solution instead of discovering a need. It is carried into RQ3 as a measured quantity.

### Surface complaint against latent need

Asked directly, candidates will say that nobody replies. That is the explicit pain point. Both statements above are latent: a candidate who cannot hold a thread open long enough to find out whether a reply was ever likely will report the problem as a reply-rate problem. Separating the two is the point of the fieldwork.

## Scope

In scope: candidates in India who pursue roles themselves on LinkedIn and ask people inside target companies for referrals, together with the people who receive those asks.

Out of scope:

| Excluded | Reason |
| --- | --- |
| Campus placement process | The placement committee controls access, so there is no design freedom, and it is a different system from self-directed outreach |
| Resume writing, interview performance, salary discussion | These sit after the stages under study |
| Job boards other than LinkedIn | The referral graph is the only reason the site matters here rather than Naukri or Indeed |
| Whether a referral improves odds | Treated as given. The question is why candidates stop pursuing one, not whether pursuing one is rational |

The scope is bounded this tightly so the journey map fits one page and every stage in it is observable inside a ten-minute Look-Ask-Try session.

## Research questions

### RQ1. How long after sending does a candidate stop, and how long does a response actually take?

Two numbers, and the finding is the gap between them. Serves Idea B. Locates the drop-off point on the journey map.

Design decision it changes: whether the intervention has to keep the candidate in the process during the silence, or whether it has to improve the asks themselves.

Falsification criterion: if candidates abandon after the median response time has already passed, they are quitting on evidence rather than prematurely. Idea B is then wrong, and the problem becomes response rate, which is a question of whom they ask and what they write.

### RQ2. At the moment an acceptance arrives days later, what does the candidate still remember and still hold?

Serves Idea A. Covers four pieces of context: who the person is, why they were chosen, which role prompted it, and what was already said.

Design decision it changes: whether the intervention belongs at the moment of acceptance, which is currently unserved, or earlier at the point of sending.

Falsification criterion: if candidates reconstruct the context from LinkedIn's own interface in under a minute and report no loss, Idea A is not a real problem and the premise about memory collapses.

### RQ3. What record do candidates keep, how much work does each company cost them, and where does the record break?

Serves both statements, and carries the manual-labour observation as a measured quantity rather than an assumption.

Design decision it changes: whether the gap is the absence of a system or the collapse of one under load. Those two findings call for opposite designs, since the first needs a system built and the second needs the existing one made cheaper to maintain.

Falsification criterion: if most candidates maintain a working record and report no loss of track, tracking is already solved and the problem moves to another stage.

### RQ4. What does the receiving side experience, and does silence mean what the candidate thinks it means?

Serves the accommodation requirement from the empathy video by putting the question to the party being imposed upon.

Design decision it changes: whether the candidate's reading of silence is accurate. If recruiters and employees accept requests and then never see the message, or would have replied given a different ask, then the problem statement is wrong about where the failure sits.

Falsification criterion, first part: if the receiving side reports that they see every message and deliberately choose not to reply, then silence does mean rejection, the candidate's reading is correct, and the design cannot improve the outcome by keeping them in the process longer.

Falsification criterion, second part: unwanted contact on LinkedIn is reported at higher rates by women than by men. Any design direction that raises cold-contact volume therefore does not affect all recipients equally. If the data reproduces that pattern it belongs in the results, not in a limitation note.

If the set has to be cut to three, RQ3 absorbs RQ2.

## Traceability rule

Every item in the questionnaire names the research question it serves. An item that cannot be tied to a design decision or to a journey-map stage is deleted at Step 3 rather than carried forward.

## Open before Step 2

1. Confirm the split between A and B, or say where the real division sits.
2. RQ1 needs a number for time-to-abandonment that respondents can recall accurately. Decide whether this is asked as elapsed days or as number of companies attempted before stopping.
3. RQ4 needs access to the receiving side. Three conversations are enough. Decide whether those are reachable or whether the question has to be answered from candidate-side evidence alone, which would weaken it.
