# 1. Research objectives and problem scope

## Problem statement: managing job-specific referral outreach on LinkedIn

A candidate pursuing referrals is not managing a list of jobs or a list of people. They are managing pairs, each target role matched with each person approached about it.

LinkedIn supports every individual action in this process. Job search, saved jobs, people search, connection invitations and messaging all exist and all work. Each sits in a separate place and carries no reference to the others, so the pairing between a vacancy and the people approached about it exists only in the candidate's head.

The load grows as a product. Five target roles with three people approached at each company gives fifteen live pairs, each in its own state: invited, accepted, messaged, replied, referred, or dead. State changes arrive from outside and days apart, so the candidate cannot schedule the work and has no signal separating a pair still pending from one that has quietly died. Reconstructing any single pair means moving between the posting, the profile, the sent-invitations list and the message thread, which is four places for one fact.

Where a company posts many similar vacancies the ambiguity compounds, because knowing that a person works there does not identify which of its roles prompted the approach.

This coordination runs alongside coursework, placement preparation and personal commitments. The effort is paid up front and any return arrives later and uncertainly. The consequences we expect to observe are stranded pairs, repeated work, follow-ups that never happen, and withdrawal from referral outreach altogether. Which of these dominates is what the fieldwork establishes.

The research problem is to establish how candidates hold this process together today, which transition most often fails, and what the coordination cost does to their ability to keep going.

## The pair state model

Each pair occupies one state. The column that matters is the last one.

| State | Advances when | Who controls the advance |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Identified | Candidate decides to approach the person | Candidate |
| Invited | Invitation accepted | Other party |
| Accepted | Candidate sends the ask | Candidate |
| Messaged | Other party replies | Other party |
| Replied | Candidate acts on the reply | Candidate |
| Referred or dead | Terminal | Either |

Three of the six transitions are controlled by the other party and carry no deadline. A pair can sit in Invited or Messaged indefinitely without any event marking it as finished, which is why stranded pairs are the expected failure and why the candidate cannot tell waiting apart from losing.

## The two ideas

Both are present in the problem statement above. They are separated here because the brief asks for the top two, and because they fail in different places and would need different interventions.

### Idea A: coordination load grows multiplicatively while capacity is fixed

The number of facts to hold is roles multiplied by people per role, each with a state and a history, distributed across four interfaces. Candidate capacity does not grow with it and is shared with everything else they are doing. Breakdown shows up as pairs stranded mid-pipeline and as work repeated because its earlier state was unrecoverable.

### Idea B: the process cannot be sustained because the signal to continue never arrives

Transitions the candidate does not control have no deadline attached, and the candidate has no base rate for what a normal reply rate or response time looks like. With no standard, silence reads as rejection. Dropping the process is free and resuming it is expensive, because resuming means rebuilding the state that Idea A explains was never stored. Breakdown shows up as abandonment, and the question is whether abandonment happens before or after the point at which a reply was still plausible.

### Why manual labour is not a third idea

The repetition across searching, identifying and contacting is real, and it is the mechanism behind both ideas rather than an idea of its own. Stated alone, tedium names its own answer, and the exercise then tests a solution instead of discovering a need. It is carried into RQ1 as a measured quantity.

### Surface complaint against latent need

Asked directly, candidates say nobody replies. That is the explicit pain point. Both ideas above are latent, and a candidate who cannot hold a pursuit together long enough to make a specific ask will experience and report the result as a reply-rate problem. Separating the two is what the fieldwork is for.

## The journey under study

1. Deciding which roles to target
2. Finding an opening worth pursuing
3. Identifying who at that company could refer
4. Sending the invitation or the ask
5. Waiting, with no signal
6. Re-engaging when a transition arrives
7. Deciding whether to continue or stop

Stage 6 is where Idea A surfaces and is the stage a feature-led analysis skips, because sending is the last thing the candidate actively does. Stage 7 is where Idea B resolves.

## Scope

In scope: candidates in India who pursue roles themselves on LinkedIn and ask people inside target companies for referrals, together with the people who receive those asks.

Out of scope:

| Excluded | Reason |
| --- | --- |
| Campus placement process | The placement committee controls access, so there is no design freedom, and it is a different system from self-directed outreach |
| Resume writing, interview performance, salary discussion | These sit after the stages under study |
| Job boards other than LinkedIn | The referral graph is the only reason the site matters here rather than Naukri or Indeed |
| Whether a referral improves the odds | Treated as given. The question is why candidates stop pursuing one |

Bounded this tightly so the journey map fits one page and every stage is observable inside a ten-minute session.

## Research questions

### RQ1. How many live pairs is a candidate carrying, and how accurately can they state the status of each without looking?

Serves Idea A. Measured as count of target roles, count of people approached per company, their product, and the number of pairs whose state the candidate can correctly report from memory.

Field task, which doubles as the Look-Ask-Try exercise: ask the participant to list every live pursuit and its status from memory, write it down, then have them open LinkedIn and reconstruct the real position. The discrepancy between the two lists is the measurement. It is recordable on video and it produces a number.

Design decision it changes: whether anything needs to be stored at all, and if so how much.

Falsification criterion: if candidates are carrying two or three pairs and report them accurately, there is no coordination problem, only a short list, and Idea A is wrong.

### RQ2. Which transition fails most often, and how long do pairs sit in each state?

Serves Idea A and supplies the journey map directly. Measured as the count of pairs stranded at each of the six states, and time in state.

The three candidate-controlled transitions and the three other-party transitions are expected to fail for different reasons, and separating them matters: a pair stranded at Accepted is the candidate's omission, a pair stranded at Invited is not.

Design decision it changes: which single transition an intervention should address.

Falsification criterion: if pairs are not stranded anywhere, there is no breakdown to study.

### RQ3. How long does a response actually take, at what point does the candidate stop, and what do they believe a normal reply rate to be?

Serves Idea B. Three quantities, and the finding is the relationship between the first two. The third is what makes silence interpretable or not.

Design decision it changes: whether the intervention has to hold the candidate in the process during the silence, or has to improve the asks themselves.

Falsification criterion: if candidates hold accurate expectations and stop only after the median response time has passed, they are acting on evidence rather than quitting early. Idea B is then wrong and the problem becomes response rate, which is a question of whom they ask and what they write.

### RQ4. What does the receiving side do with these asks, and what would have made them act?

Serves the accommodation requirement from the empathy video by putting the question to the party being imposed upon. It also tests whether the specificity lost in Idea A is worth anything.

Design decision it changes: whether recovering the role and the context improves the outcome, or whether referral decisions are made on grounds the candidate cannot influence.

Falsification criterion, first part: if recipients report that they refer only people they already know, regardless of how specific the ask is, then no improvement to coordination changes the outcome and both ideas need rethinking.

Falsification criterion, second part: unwanted contact on LinkedIn is reported at higher rates by women than by men. Any design direction that raises cold-contact volume therefore does not affect all recipients equally. If the data reproduces that pattern it belongs in the results, not a limitation note.

## Mechanics to confirm during fieldwork

Observed, not asserted on a slide:

- Whether the invitation note is visible to the sender after acceptance, and from where
- What the sent-invitations list shows, and how many clicks it takes to reach
- What an acceptance notification carries beyond name and headline
- What remains retrievable once a posting is closed or withdrawn
- Whether a saved job can be associated with a person in any way

## Traceability rule

Every item in the questionnaire names the research question it serves. An item that cannot be tied to a design decision or a journey-map stage is deleted at Step 3 rather than carried forward.

## Open before Step 2

1. Confirm the split between Idea A and Idea B, or say where the real division sits.
2. RQ3 needs a recall measure for abandonment. Elapsed days is the truer variable and the worse recalled. Companies attempted before stopping is recalled well and is a usable proxy. Asking both and reporting the discrepancy is itself a finding.
3. RQ4 needs three conversations on the receiving end. Employees at any company who have been approached by strangers count the same as recruiters and are easier to reach. If three are not reachable the question is answered from candidate-side evidence alone and is weaker for it.
